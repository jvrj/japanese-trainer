**Action.** Run the 50-lesson crawl into a cached JSON spine before any more frame-inventory research. Rate-limit it, store the raw HTML, and extract pattern strings only — discard the site's English glosses at ingest time so they cannot leak into content later.

### H2. [UNVERIFIED-EVIDENCE] The ほうがいい N5-vs-N4 disagreement is a syllabus-position artefact, not a source error — Genki I Ch.12 (Vol I) vs MNN Lesson 32 (Vol II)

*Lanes: eng-hou-ga-ii, eng-grammar-data, eng-syllabus-order, eng-closed-sets, synthesis-refetch*

**What.** Four lanes reported a level disagreement on the advice frame and none could explain it. The N5 camp is large and consistent — JLPT Sensei, Japanesetest4you, Coto Academy, Japanese Ammo all label 〜ほうがいい as N5. The dissent is Hanabira, which files V-た ほうがいい under N4, and Maggie Sensei, which hedges to 'N4–N5'. The MNN crawl resolves it. The advice frame sits in Genki I Chapter 12 — inside Genki Volume I, the N5 book, which I re-verified directly against the St. Olaf index (Ch.12 = -desho, ho ga ii desu, -n-desu, -nakucha-ikemasen, -node, -sugiru). It also sits in Minna no Nihongo Lesson 32 — inside MNN Volume II, which is the N4 volume (Vol I = lessons 1-25 = N5, Vol II = 26-50 = N4). Both placements are correct; the references simply inherit whichever syllabus they were built against. MNN L32 teaches both polarities together as one point: 'the verb form た / the verb form ない + ほうがいいです', glossed as advising someone to do or not do something for their own good, with examples まいにちうんどうしたほうがいいです and じゃ、おふろにはいらないほうがいいですよ. The comparison frame draws no such dispute — it is N5 everywhere. Critically, BOTH syllabi teach the comparison ほう before the advice ほう: Genki Ch.10 (Comparison, verified verbatim at source) precedes Ch.12, and MNN L12 (comparative より / どちらが / いちばん) precedes L32.

**Why it matters.** This directly answers the lens's named open item — the learner reports ほうがいい at school and the prior 40-frame table had no row for it — and it answers it with something better than a row. The 'both agree comparison comes first' convergence is the actionable part: two independently-authored syllabi, built decades apart for different markets, both put the comparison sense of ほう before the advice sense. That is a real ordering signal for drill design, not a coincidence to be argued about. It also means the app should never introduce the advice frame to a learner who has not met the comparison frame, because neither classroom does. Secondarily, the level column on the frame table has to become plural — a single jlpt_level field will silently pick a winner in exactly the cases the lens asked us not to.

**Integration.** Add both ほう frames as distinct rows with distinct frame_ids (advice_v_ta_hou_ga_ii, comparison_a_yori_b_no_hou_ga), never one row with two senses — the shared morpheme is precisely what makes them confusable, so the data model should hold them apart even though the lexeme is shared. Replace the scalar level field with jlpt_level_by_source (an array of {source, level}) so the N5/N4 split is carried rather than collapsed. Gate the advice frame behind the comparison frame in any ordering logic. Since the learner cannot read kanji, every stored example must be kana: たべたほうがいいです / たべないほうがいいです for advice, すしのほうがすきです for comparison.

**Devil's advocate.** The syllabus-position explanation is an inference I am drawing across two sources, not a claim either source makes — no fetched page says 'Hanabira says N4 because it follows MNN'. Hanabira may simply have made a judgement call about difficulty, or follow a third syllabus entirely. The explanation is well-supported and parsimonious but it is reconstruction, and it should be recorded as such. Separately, the MNN Vol I = N5 / Vol II = N4 split is itself a conventional mapping reported by lanes rather than an official JLPT statement — the JLPT does not certify textbooks. And note the ordering convergence is slightly weaker than it looks: Genki Ch.10 'Comparison' is documented to include の方が, whereas the MNN L12 extraction lists より, どちらが and いちばん without explicitly naming の方が, so 'comparison precedes advice' holds in both, but 'の方が specifically precedes ほうがいい' is confirmed only for Genki.

**Action.** Add both frames as separate rows with a plural level field; order comparison before advice; and ask the learner one diagnostic question — which textbook the class uses and whether they have already done the より comparison — since on MNN, reaching ほうがいい means they are 32 lessons in and well into N4 material, which would reset assumptions about what else they already have.

### H3. [UNVERIFIED-EVIDENCE] Position words: Genki's 9 and MNN's 10 are DIFFERENT SETS, not different counts — and the lane that called nine 'a fantasy' made a category error

*Lanes: eng-closed-sets, eng-hou-ga-ii, eng-grammar-data, eng-syllabus-order, synthesis-refetch*

**What.** This was the sharpest lane-vs-lane contradiction in the scan and I re-fetched the primary page to settle it. eng-grammar-data asserted that 'no sources propose a 9-word set; the prior pass's reference to nine position words likely conflates these two 4-word series into a 9-word fantasy. Actual closed set: 8 words total' — where its 8 are ここ/そこ/あそこ/どこ plus こちら/そちら/あちら/どちら. That is a category error. Those are kosoado PLACE and DIRECTION demonstratives; the position words at issue are relative-position NOUNS used in the frame X は Y の ___ です. They are different closed sets that coexist. Direct re-fetch of St. Olaf's Genki I Ch.4 location-words page returns exactly nine, verbatim: みぎ・ひだり・まえ・うしろ・なか・うえ・した・そば・となり. The real and far more useful disagreement is between the syllabi. MNN Lesson 10 teaches ten, and the membership differs: うえ・した・まえ・うしろ・あいだ・そと・ひだり・みぎ・ちかく・となり. Genki has なか and そば; MNN L10 does not, and instead carries あいだ・そと・ちかく. Seven words are common to both; Genki adds 2, MNN adds 3. Coto Academy separately extends to 13 by adding そと・よこ・あいだ・ちかく to the core 8-9, which is a superset that reconciles both — and LearnJapaneseAZ's standalone article is titled '9 Japanese Position Words' but only enumerates 8 in its body, which is likely where the '8 vs 9' confusion originated.

**Why it matters.** The lens asked specifically for exact membership with documented disagreement, and this is the cleanest instance in the whole scan: two classroom syllabi, two closed sets, overlapping but not identical, both citable. It also has direct product consequence. A 'finish the set' mechanic — which is the entire appeal of a closed slot for a learner — needs a definite membership, and the honest answer is that membership is syllabus-dependent. Shipping Genki's 9 to a learner whose class teaches MNN's 10 means they will meet あいだ・そと・ちかく in class and find them missing from the app, which reads as the app being incomplete. The union of 12 is the safer ship.

**Integration.** Store position words as a union set of 12 (みぎ・ひだり・まえ・うしろ・なか・うえ・した・そば・となり・あいだ・そと・ちかく) with per-word provenance tags (genki_ch4, mnn_l10, coto_extended), so a round can be filtered to whichever syllabus the learner's class follows and the completion meter counts against the right denominator. This fits the existing literal-categories rule in this repo — the tile is position words and every member is literally a position word. Kana only: うえ・した・なか・まえ・うしろ・みぎ・ひだり・となり・そば・あいだ・そと・ちかく.

**Devil's advocate.** The MNN membership comes from one secondary source whose glosses in that very list are wrong (it renders あいだ as 'inside, middle' when あいだ is 'between', and まえ as 'previous', うしろ as 'after' — plausible for the time senses, misleading for the spatial frame being taught). The membership list is much more likely to be faithfully transcribed from the textbook than the glosses are to be correct, but it is still a single witness and deserves a second before the set is treated as settled. Also, presenting a 12-word union to the learner arguably defeats the closed-set psychology the lens is built on: a set you can finish is motivating precisely because its edge is fixed, and a union with provenance tags has a softer edge than either syllabus's set. If the learner's textbook can be identified, shipping exactly that syllabus's set is the stronger product choice and the union becomes internal data only.

**Action.** Ship the 12-word union with provenance tags but default the visible set to the learner's actual syllabus once identified; fix the glosses from a reliable reference, never from learnjapaneseaz. Correct the eng-grammar-data claim in any downstream artefact — nine is verified at source, and the 8-word kosoado set is a separate closed set that deserves its own row.

Chapter 5 is: Adjectives, Counting, Degree expressions, masho (with suki/kirai listed by the other lanes) — there is no ほうがいい and no でしょう. That same lane also produced the refuted position-word claim in HIGH #3. Two confident, load-bearing, wrong claims from one lane, both caught only because the contradiction with other lanes was visible enough to prompt a re-fetch.

Chapter ordering is exactly the kind of value nobody re-checks once it is in a table, and a wrong chapter number silently corrupts any 'what has the class covered' gating built on top of it. The prior pass's 40-frame table was produced the same way and carries the same risk: it was verified against JLPT Sensei and the St. Olaf index, but verified how, and re-checked by whom.

**Integration.** Before the 40-frame table is used to generate any drill content, re-derive its two ordering columns mechanically: pull Genki chapters from the St. Olaf index and MNN lessons from the crawl in HIGH #1, both by fetch, and diff the result against the existing table. ## MEDIUM

### M1. Recombining frames: five clause-embedding families, with classroom unlock points now verified in BOTH syllabi

*Lanes: eng-recombination, eng-syllabus-order, eng-closed-sets, synthesis-refetch*

**What.** Lens item 4 asked which frames take whole other frames as slot fillers. Five families recur across lanes: relative clauses (a verb/adjective clause modifying a following noun — head-final, the opposite of English), reason clauses (から / ので / し), sequencing (て-form linking, てから, まえに, あとで), quotation (と いいます / と おもいます), and conditionals (たら / ば / と / なら). Relative clauses and reason clauses are the productive ones — their slot is an arbitrary clause, so they multiply the utterance space rather than adding to it; quotation is similar in kind. Classroom unlock points are now dual-sourced: relative clauses are Genki I Ch.9 (qualifying nouns with verbs and adjectives) and MNN Lesson 22 (noun modification, verified verbatim this pass, including the が-in-subordinate-clause rule and the dictionary-form + じかん/ようじ/やくそく pattern); reason から is Genki Ch.6 with a second explanatory sense at Ch.9 and ので at Ch.12; て-form is Genki Ch.6 with sentence-joining at Ch.7; とき is Genki II Ch.16; まえに/てから Ch.17; conditionals split たら at Ch.17, と at Ch.18, ば at Ch.22, なら at Ch.13.

**Why it matters.** Recombination is what turns a finite frame list into unbounded production, and it is the mechanism by which a drill app stops being a phrasebook. The unlock points matter because a recombining frame is only drillable once its filler frames exist — there is no point offering a reason-clause drill before the learner has clauses to put in it. Both syllabi place relative clauses at roughly the same relative depth (Genki 9 of 23, MNN 22 of 50), which is a mild convergence signal that this is genuinely where clause-embedding becomes teachable.

That single field makes the 40-frame table generative instead of enumerative, and lets a round compose two frames the learner already has rather than introducing a new one.

**Devil's advocate.** No fetched source anywhere in this scan provides the actual 2D matrix the lens asked for — [frame] x [can this frame fill that slot] — and three lanes said so independently. **Action.** Add a slot-type field with a clause option to the frame schema now; defer the nesting matrix until the frame spine is stable, and build it from a grammar reference (Tsujimura, Shibatani) rather than more web search.

### M3. [UNVERIFIED-EVIDENCE] The advice frame's verb-form slot is genuinely closed (た / ない) — a two-way choice, and MNN teaches both polarities as one point

*Lanes: eng-hou-ga-ii, eng-closed-sets, eng-grammar-data, synthesis-refetch*

**What.** Within V-___ ほうがいい the verb itself is open (any verb) but the conjugation slot is closed to two productive forms: past affirmative た for the assertive recommendation, and plain negative ない for the caution. A dictionary-form variant る is attested as a softer suggestion but is described as rare. The asymmetry is the teachable bit and it trips learners: the affirmative takes PAST tense while the negative stays present — たべたほうがいい but たべないほうがいい, never たべなかったほうがいい. MNN Lesson 32 confirms both polarities are introduced together as a single grammar point ('the verb form た / the verb form ない + ほうがいいです'), which is direct evidence the two-way contrast is the unit of instruction rather than two separate things. Register note from the lane: ほうがいい is a direct recommendation, softer than べき (obligation) and firmer than たらいい / といい (suggestion).

**Why it matters.** A closed two-way slot inside an otherwise open frame is the ideal shape for a drill — the learner picks た or ない against a prompt, which is a decidable binary rather than open production, and the た/ない tense asymmetry is a concrete error the drill can actually catch. It is also the frame the learner is being taught at school right now, so it has immediate relevance rather than theoretical.

**Integration.** Drill it as a polarity choice over an open verb slot: fixed frame, learner selects the form. Kana throughout — いったほうがいいです / いかないほうがいいです. The tense asymmetry is the one rule the card should surface, since it is the predictable mistake.

**Devil's advocate.** The 'closed to 2-3 forms' characterisation comes from pedagogical sources describing what is taught, not from corpus evidence about what is said, and no fetched source gives frequency data for た vs ない vs る — the lens explicitly forbids inventing such a statistic, so the る form's rarity is a reported description and nothing stronger. The nuance claim that た is 'stronger' than る is also a single-lane synthesis across grammar blogs, not a sourced contrast.

**Action.** Build the polarity drill on the た/ない binary; state the tense asymmetry explicitly on the card; do not claim relative frequency of the three forms anywhere in content.

### M4. [UNVERIFIED-EVIDENCE] The comparison frame's の rule: nouns take の方が, verbs take 方が bare — a concrete production error to drill

*Lanes: eng-hou-ga-ii, eng-syllabus-order*

**What.** In A より B の方が + adjective, the の is obligatory when B is a noun (すしのほうがすきです) and dropped when B is a verb in dictionary form (かくほうがむずかしいです). Two sources state this rule explicitly. Word order is flexible — either element may lead — and より can be dropped entirely when the comparison target is clear from context (このかばんのほうがやすいです). Both noun slots and the adjective slot are fully open.

**Why it matters.** It is a small, decidable, high-frequency rule of exactly the kind a drill catches better than a classroom does, and it sits inside a frame both syllabi teach early (Genki Ch.10, MNN L12). It also gives the comparison frame a closed sub-decision, which otherwise it lacks — all three of its main slots being open makes it hard to drill as anything but free production.

**Integration.** A の / no-の toggle drill over a fixed frame with the filler type varying between noun and verb. Kana: ラーメンよりうどんのほうがすきです vs よむよりかくほうがむずかしいです.

**Devil's advocate.** Sourced from two grammar sites rather than a textbook index, and the no-の-with-verbs rule is described by one lane as having usage variation in practice; treat it as the taught rule rather than an absolute. Also, MNN L12's comparative as extracted teaches the より form with どちらが and いちばん and does not explicitly surface の方が, so this frame's MNN lesson placement is not actually pinned yet.

**Action.** Include the の rule as a drillable sub-decision; pin the MNN lesson for の方が specifically when the 50-lesson crawl runs.
